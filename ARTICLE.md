# Six GitHub Accounts, Four Independent Signals, and the Point Where I Stopped Digging

## The follower I almost ignored

It started the way most of these do: a notification I nearly swiped away.

A new follower, a profile with a stock-photo avatar and a bio that read like it had been generated by something that had read a thousand developer bios and averaged them.

Not alarming on its own — GitHub is full of these.

What made me stop was that it reminded me of a shape I'd seen before.

Almost a year earlier I'd investigated a wave of nine accounts that spun up 552 repositories in 34 minutes. Same fallback README text across every one. Same co-author trailer stitched into commit after commit:

`Hajigur <66867581+hajigur69@users.noreply.github.com>`

I'd published that investigation, moved on, and mostly forgotten about it.

Now here was a follower whose account carried the same texture — the same generic scaffolding, the same rushed-looking history.

And then another.

And another.

The obvious read is:

> "The botnet is still running, and it followed me."

The obvious read is also, on its own, worth almost nothing.

Six accounts *looking* related is not the same as six accounts *being* related, and I've spent enough time in this space to know that pattern-matching on vibes is how false attributions get published.

So instead of writing that up, I did something slower.

I asked what it would actually take to make that claim defensible, and I tried to build a version of the investigation that would still hold up if I were hostile to my own conclusion.

## Why "it looks similar" isn't evidence

Here's the trap.

Six accounts with generic bios, generic READMEs, and the same rough commit cadence *feel* coordinated.

But developers copy each other's scaffolding constantly. READMEs converge because everyone's read the same three "how to write a good README" posts.

Bot-like *vibes* are not a measurement.

And if I built a case out of vibes, anyone could tear it down by finding one ordinary account that also fit the profile.

So I set myself a harder bar before touching any data:

**Decide what would count as evidence, and what threshold it would need to clear, before I looked.**

If I picked thresholds after seeing the numbers, I'd inevitably pick ones the data happened to satisfy.

That's not analysis.

That's storytelling with extra steps.

Every test in this investigation got a frozen protocol first: what's being measured, what counts as a pass, what counts as a fail.

Only then did I run it.

That discipline is unglamorous, and it also killed several early leads. A few things I was fairly confident would pan out — didn't.

I'll get to those, because they matter as much as the things that worked.

## Signal one: the repos didn't just get created, they got created *in order*

The six accounts' repositories weren't just similar in structure — their creation timestamps fell into a consistent cross-account sequence, again and again.

Across 302 consecutive transitions in the frozen dense run, **297 followed the expected next account**.

That's **98.34%**.

The sequence looked like this:

```text
KevinWong455
→ Seo-yeonLee
→ ElvinHasanov3d
→ badiranssen
→ RashidSaeedAlMazrouei18
→ 9Michael-Cooley
→ repeat
```

Five transitions deviated.

I kept them.

A later, independently collected provenance dataset reproduced the same structure from oldest commits: **318 of 350 owner transitions** followed that already-frozen cycle.

That's **90.86%**.

In that same dataset, **350 of 351 repositories received their oldest observed commit within two seconds of repository creation**.

That's important because the second dataset wasn't used to invent a new pattern.

The cycle already existed.

The later measurement asked whether a different observable — oldest-commit ordering — reproduced it.

It did.

That's a behavioral signature: something is producing these repositories in a process that treats the six accounts as slots in one sequence, not six people independently deciding to push code.

On its own, this strongly supports coordinated tooling or automation — a generator, a script, a template pipeline.

It doesn't establish shared *ownership*, and it doesn't pin down the mechanism.

Plenty of legitimate automation looks orderly too.

## Signal two: they were all following the same ~20,000 accounts

Independent of the repo timing, I pulled the outbound follow-lists — who each of these accounts follows, not who follows them.

Among the four accounts whose captured following sets reconciled cleanly to their profile counts, the overlap in *who they followed* was startling.

The three larger sets, each around 34,000 accounts, had pairwise Jaccard similarity between **0.9895 and 0.9906**.

Roughly 99% overlap.

The smaller fourth set was between **98.6% and 99.1% contained** within each of the larger ones.

The authoritative four-way intersection — accounts followed by all four reconciled sets — contained:

**20,067 GitHub accounts.**

That degree of overlap is difficult to reconcile with independent organic target selection.

It's strongly consistent with a shared acquisition process — something driving these accounts toward the same follow targets, whatever that something turns out to be.

But this measurement also produced failures.

Two accounts failed the original completeness check and were excluded.

Each then received exactly one stricter, predeclared double-pass retry.

Both retries failed closed.

So neither account was added back into the authoritative overlap result.

That means I'm **not** claiming a clean six-account following snapshot.

I'm **not** claiming a six-way intersection.

The authoritative result is four accounts, because those are the four sets that passed the frozen rules.

A signal this strong doesn't need help from cherry-picking.

Pretending every check succeeded would make the whole thing less trustworthy, not more.

## Signal three: the exact same bytes, reused across owners

This is the one that moved me from "probably coordinated" to "confident these accounts share technical inputs."

Git doesn't just track file contents.

Every unique blob — a file's content in Git's object model — has a content-addressed hash.

Two files with byte-for-byte identical content produce the *identical* Git blob SHA, regardless of who authored them or which repository contains them.

Across 351 public repositories, I found **ten blob SHAs reused across repositories owned by different accounts in the six-account cohort**.

Six of those ten appeared under *all six* account namespaces.

The same exact file content, repeatedly present across repositories belonging to six nominally separate identities.

Some of the reused objects appeared at paths such as:

- `.gitignore`
- `tsconfig.json`
- `.env.example`
- `src/App.css`

What this establishes is narrower than it might sound.

It proves exact shared file content.

It does **not** prove who copied it, what generated it, or whether the accounts share repository history.

In fact, the provenance analysis found **no cross-owner tree SHA reuse**.

So I'm not claiming the repositories themselves are identical or that they share the same Git history.

And exact blob reuse alone can absolutely come from shared templates or tooling. Legitimate scaffolding systems emit identical `.gitignore`, configuration, and boilerplate files all the time.

What made this interesting wasn't the reuse in isolation.

It was the repeated cross-owner reuse across the same six-account cohort, alongside the independently measured sequencing and targeting structure from signals one and two.

That gives us a different kind of evidence from timing or follow behavior.

Not just similar behavior.

Exact technical reuse.

## Signal four: three pairs that became exact under a rule frozen in advance

The last signal came from commit-author emails — specifically, the local part, the bit before the `@`.

Raw, these six email strings looked different: different punctuation layouts and no obvious exact relationship.

The moment I noticed that they *looked* related, I stopped.

I didn't start trying transformations until something matched.

Instead, I froze one normalization rule before scoring anything:

1. lowercase the local part;
2. remove every character that isn't a letter or digit;
3. compare the resulting strings exactly.

Then I checked all **15 possible pairings** among the six identities.

Three pairs collapsed into exact matches.

Not "similar."

Identical, character for character, under the frozen normalization.

And all three exact pairs also corresponded to edges in the repository-creation cycle that had already been established independently through signal one.

The matching edges were:

```text
Seo-yeonLee → ElvinHasanov3d
badiranssen → RashidSaeedAlMazrouei18
9Michael-Cooley → KevinWong455
```

That last one is the wrap-around edge of the six-account cycle.

The three alternating cycle edges were non-matches, with edit distances of:

```text
9
11
9
```

That doesn't prove the emails were generated sequentially.

I didn't define a background-population model that would let me assign a clean probability to seeing three exact normalized pairs, so I'm not inventing one after the fact.

The result is descriptive and structural:

**three disjoint pairs became exact under a normalization rule frozen before analysis, and those same pairs aligned with edges in a separately established account cycle.**

I also tested a narrower competing explanation.

What if the email identities were simply derived from the visible GitHub usernames?

I normalized the handles, calculated the real owner-to-email similarity assignment, and then enumerated all:

**6! = 720 possible assignments.**

The real assignment ranked:

**369 out of 720**

with an exact one-sided:

**p = 0.53**

Essentially unremarkable.

So this measurement gave me **no evidence that the visible GitHub usernames predict the normalized email local parts**.

That doesn't prove no handle-derived process exists.

It means this particular hypothesis wasn't supported.

Either way, it doesn't touch the three exact normalized pairs themselves — those stand regardless of how the username test came out.

I'm calling this signal *identity-generation morphology*, deliberately.

Not "an identity match."

I did **not** find an exact cross-owner author `(name, email)` bridge.

This is a structural echo, not a fingerprint match.

Collapsing it into the stronger claim would be exactly the kind of overreach I was trying to avoid from the start.

## Why four different signals matter more than one dramatic one

Any single one of these observations has an alternative explanation.

Shared scaffolding tools can produce highly regular repository activity.

Accounts drawing from the same discovery source can develop overlapping follow-lists.

Template files get copied, and scaffolding tools can emit identical content.

Coincidences happen in large datasets.

What gets harder to explain is all four patterns appearing in the same six-account cohort, on different observables, measured with different methods.

Repository timing could turn out to have some alternate explanation and the blob-hash result would still exist.

The blob reuse could theoretically come from common templates or scaffolding tools, and the outbound-follow overlap would still independently show approximately 99% target-set similarity among the three large reconciled sets.

The email morphology could be coincidence, and it still wouldn't erase the repository sequence.

Each layer is a separate load-bearing wall.

I haven't built a formal joint-probability model proving these measurements are statistically independent.

So I'm not multiplying probabilities and handing you some dramatic:

> "one in a trillion"

number.

That would be a stronger claim than I can back.

The narrower point is enough:

**four differently constructed measurements — including negative controls, exclusions, and failed validations — converged on the same operational explanation.**

That's triangulation.

Not accumulation.

## What I'm not claiming

This is the part I think matters most, and it's usually the part that gets cut for length.

So I'm not cutting it.

I did **not** find an exact match between any two accounts' full `(name, email)` commit-author identity.

I did **not** find any shared repository tree or shared Git history.

I did **not** find evidence that visible GitHub usernames meaningfully predict the email identities behind them.

I have **no** evidence of shared credentials.

I have **no** evidence identifying a single human operator.

I have **no** evidence of shared IP or network infrastructure.

I have **no** evidence identifying a specific commercial service or account vendor.

I have **no** evidence that my earlier publication caused this cluster to follow me, as opposed to broad automated discovery or some wider follow-for-star ecosystem.

And I have **no** basis for turning technical coordination into a claim about malicious intent beyond the behavior actually observed.

The bounded claim, and the only claim I'm actually making, is this:

**These six accounts are very likely driven by a shared technical orchestration or generation system.**

Common human ownership is not established.

Deliberate targeting of me specifically is not established.

Publication causation is not established.

I don't get to round any of those up just because the mechanical-coordination case is strong.

## Closing the investigation, on purpose

I formally declared this collection phase closed.

Not "paused."

Not "I'll keep checking until something interesting appears."

Closed.

Every source run, artifact, PR, protocol, and commit feeding the conclusion is frozen in the investigation record.

Two failed measurements stay failed.

Excluded accounts stay excluded from the measurements they didn't pass.

Normalization rules don't change because another transformation might produce a prettier result.

If I want to extend the investigation later, the rule I'm holding myself to is the same one that got me here:

**write the new research question and the new pass/fail protocol before looking at the new evidence.**

Because there's another failure mode in investigations like this that doesn't get talked about enough.

Once you've found something real, it becomes incredibly easy to keep testing until you've built a story around it.

Every additional metric gives you another chance to discover something interesting.

It also gives you another chance to overfit.

At some point, stopping becomes part of the methodology.

It's tempting to keep pulling threads once you've found four that hold.

I think the discipline of stopping is part of what makes the four that did hold worth trusting.
